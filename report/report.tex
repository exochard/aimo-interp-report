\begin{abstract}
A language model that solves a mathematics problem may fail once the problem is reworded, given typos or padded with an irrelevant sentence. The AIMO (AI Mathematical Olympiad) Interpretability Challenge asks, for a given model and problem, whether the model's accuracy survives such answer-preserving changes. We audit the challenge's labelled data and test six predictors computed from the model. First, the labels depend mainly on the problem: 13 of the 14 problems labelled for several models carry the same label for every model, against 4.8 expected by chance, so validation that keeps a problem's other labels in training rewards recognising the problem. Second, on a separate labelled set the organisers released for one model, problem length alone outperforms the organisers' own example solution under that solution's own training procedure, because the set's ``change of setting'' perturbations are all labelled non-robust and are applied to short problems; on the competition labels length carries no signal. Third, holding out whole problems, none of six signals computed from the model gains more than two of 34 problem-model pairs over a matched constant answer.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\textbf{The task.} The challenge, run alongside the AI Mathematical Olympiad (AIMO) and hosted on the Codabench competition platform, concerns models that solve competition mathematics. For a model $M$ and a problem $P$, the organisers wrote perturbed versions of $P$ with the same answer (typos, an irrelevant sentence, renamed people or variables, rephrasing, a change of setting, and expert-written edits), sampled $M$ on the original and on every version (temperature 1.0, up to 100,000 tokens), and labelled the pair $(M, P)$ \emph{robust} if $M$'s accuracy holds on every version and \emph{non-robust} if it drops clearly on at least one; pairs in between carry no label (competition FAQ, items 7, 12 and 13; AIMO Interpretability Challenge organisers, 2026). A participant submits code that may run $M$ on $P$, within 3,600 seconds for all models and problems together, and must output one of the two labels; it is scored by accuracy on problems the participant never sees. The small-model track uses four open models of 4 to 8 billion parameters (Qwen3.5-4B, Skywork-OR1-Math-7B, Olmo-3-7B-Think and DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B); the main track adds gpt-oss-120b at three reasoning-effort settings. The organisers release training labels (the Hugging Face dataset \texttt{train-main-v2}), auxiliary DeepSeek data, and a public sample, and score submissions on a public and a private test set.

\textbf{Our question.} Does any \emph{signal}, a quantity computed from the model's forward passes or generations on a problem, predict the label on a problem the predictor has not seen? We call one labelled (model, problem) pair a \emph{row}. We find:

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Labels follow the problem, so row-level validation leaks} (Section 4.1). In the organisers' latest label release, 13 of the 14 problems labelled for several models, gpt-oss-120b included, carry one label for every model, against 4.8 expected when labels are shuffled across rows (2,000 shuffles, 95 \% range 2 to 8; none reaches 13). If a problem's other rows stay in training, copying their label gains up to 8 of 43 rows over a constant answer.
\item
  \textbf{A baseline's reported score can come from its dataset} (Section 4.2). Under the organisers' own selection protocol, problem length reaches balanced accuracy 0.70 on their auxiliary DeepSeek set, against 0.63 reported for their uncertainty-based example solution. Every ``change of setting'' row of that set is non-robust and these problems are short; on the competition labels, length predicts nothing.
\item
  \textbf{Six signals show no gain on unseen problems} (Section 4.3). For three of them the success criterion was committed before any result existed, and none met its criterion. The best signal, ability calibration, is one row above the constant answer ``robust'' on 34 rows (two after a reviewer's correction), with an interval that includes zero.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Data and evaluation protocol}

\textbf{Labels.} We use the organisers' \texttt{is\_robust} field. Its exact threshold is unpublished and leaves a gap between the classes. The labels also carry \texttt{max\_drop}, the largest drop in accuracy over the perturbation types. Our experiments were frozen on the 82-row version of \texttt{train-main-v2} (dataset revision \texttt{23791d9b}); its 34 labelled small-track rows cover 20 problems (Qwen3.5-4B 12, DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B 10, Olmo-3-7B-Think 9, Skywork-OR1-Math-7B 3), and 24 are robust. The 15 October release (94 rows) has 43 labelled small-track rows over 22 problems; we refit everything on it as a second check. The organisers also release, for DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B alone, accuracies before and after each perturbation with robustness labels: the \emph{augmented set} (141 rows, 137 problems) and its \emph{filtered subset} (54 rows, 52 problems), together the \emph{auxiliary} DeepSeek data. All labels are the organisers'.

\textbf{Problem-held-out evaluation.} Each problem contributes up to four rows, one per model. The outer loop leaves out one whole problem at a time (20 folds), fits on the rest and predicts the held-out rows. Every choice a method makes (feature scaling, regularisation strength, feature set) is made on the training problems only, by an inner leave-one-problem-out loop.

\textbf{Baseline and metrics.} The \emph{majority baseline} answers ``robust'' for every row: 24 of 34 correct (accuracy 0.706). The public test sets are about half robust (always ``robust'' scores 0.48 on the main track and 0.50 on the small track), so we also report balanced accuracy, the mean recall over the two classes, for which any constant answer scores 0.500. Differences from the baseline come with 95 \% intervals from 10,000 bootstrap resamples of whole problems.

\textbf{Success criteria committed in advance.} For five signals we committed to git a criterion the signal had to meet, all conditions together (Table~\ref{tab:criteria}). For the prompt-only, trace and hidden-activation signals the commit precedes every result file. For calibration, a first file of per-variant results was written 6 seconds before the commit, and the nested selection we report was added 90 minutes later, after that first result. For forced-answer flips, the commit follows the output file of a first run, which produced no answers, by 25 seconds (Appendix B.1).

\section{Methods}

Every signal sees only what a submitted solution could see: the problem text, and the model's own forward passes and generations on it. All predictors are linear and regularised, because 34 rows cannot support more. A logistic regression predicts robust at a probability of 0.5 or more; its inverse regularisation strength $C$ (smaller is stronger) is chosen on the training problems unless stated otherwise. Our sampling uses temperature 0.6 and top-p 0.95.

\textbf{Prompt-only features.} The model reads the original problem and two perturbed copies, one with letter swaps in words outside the mathematical expressions and one with an irrelevant sentence added. Each copy gets one forward pass and no generation. The \emph{capability} pair is the per-token negative log-likelihood (NLL) of the original prompt and its log length in tokens. The six \emph{sensitivity} features are, for each copy, the change in NLL and the cosine distance between the hidden states of original and copy, averaged over prompt tokens, at 50 \% and 75 \% of the layers. A logistic regression with fixed $C = 1$ reads the capability pair, the sensitivity six, or all eight (\emph{combined}). A model that is surprised or moved by surface edits might be fragile on that problem.

\textbf{Ability calibration.} Robustness might simply be a strong model meeting an easy problem. The \emph{ability} feature of a row is its model's robust rate over the training rows of other problems, $(\sum y + k\,p_0)/(n + k)$ on the logit scale, where the sum runs over that model's $n$ training labels $y \in \{0, 1\}$, $p_0$ is the robust rate of all fitting rows (for a fitting row this includes its own label; Section 4.3 reports the run without it), and $k$ sets how strongly small counts are pulled towards $p_0$. A held-out problem has no labels, so its difficulty can only be estimated from the same model's prompt NLL and log length. A logistic regression is offered five feature sets: ability; ability with the problem's robust rate under the other models; ability with NLL and length; ability with all three; and those with the six sensitivity features added. The problem's own rate does not exist for a held-out problem, so three sets are distinct under problem holdout; the inner loop picks the set, $k \in \{1, 4, 16\}$ and $C \in \{0.1, 1, 10\}$ together.

\textbf{Generation stability.} Qwen3.5-4B samples four solutions to the original problem and to each of the two copies above, up to 8,192 tokens each. We compute statistics of the original samples (agreement with their most common answer, share unanswered, share cut off at the token limit, mean length) and, for each copy, how often its answers equal the original's most common answer, which needs no answer key, the drop in that agreement, the relative change in length, and the change in the samples' mean token log-probability, plus the largest drop and smallest agreement over the two copies. We correlate each of the 13 that vary across rows with \texttt{max\_drop}; a threshold on the best one, chosen after seeing all 13 by leave-one-out on the 12 labelled Qwen3.5-4B rows, gives a prediction. This signal is exploratory and has no success criterion.

\textbf{Counterfactual trace sensitivity.} The model writes the first 256 tokens of its reasoning on the original problem (greedy decoding). We keep that text fixed and compute its per-token log-likelihood when it follows a rewritten problem instead of the original. A script produces four kinds of rewrite meant to keep the answer: variables renamed (also inside the text), equivalent numeric expressions, the two sides of an equation swapped, and givens reordered. It checks that renamings and numeric edits can be reversed exactly and that a reordering only permutes the original givens; it does not check that reordered givens are independent. A \emph{numeric-edit control} raises one integer constant by one, which usually changes the answer; we did not verify that it always does. Eleven features measure the response:
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item the text's mean log-probability under the original problem;
\item the change in per-token log-likelihood under the rewrites: mean, the largest mean change over rewrite types, variance, and the share falling on the least likely 20 \% of tokens;
\item the cosine change of hidden states, mean over tokens at 50 \% depth and 90th percentile at 75 \% depth, and at 75 \% on tokens matching an edited name or number;
\item the ratio of the mean log-likelihood change under rewrites to that under the control;
\item indicators for a missing rewrite type or control.
\end{itemize}
Reasoning tied to surface form should react to harmless rewrites almost as much as to the control. An L2-regularised logistic regression with a per-model intercept reads the features.

\textbf{Hidden-activation probe.} For each row we take the model's hidden states over the prompt and over the first 128 tokens of its greedy reasoning, at 25, 50 and 75 \% of the layers, each summarised by the mean over tokens and by the last token: twelve vectors. Different models have different hidden spaces, so similarity is defined only within a model: the mean cosine similarity over the twelve vectors plus a constant 0.25, and zero across models. A kernel ridge regression on this similarity, fitted to $\pm 1$ targets with its penalty chosen on the training problems, predicts robust when its output is at least 0.

\textbf{Forced-answer flips.} This signal approximates what the label measures, whether answers change under perturbation, at the scale a submission can afford. The model solves the original and two or three perturbed copies (typos, an irrelevant sentence, and renamed people where the problem has any), four samples each, up to 2,048 tokens. A sample that has not finished is closed: we append the end-of-reasoning marker and ``The final answer is \textbackslash boxed\{'' and decode 24 more tokens greedily. Four statistics follow: the share of answers on the copies that differ from the most common original answer (mean and worst copy), the share of original answers that agree with each other, and the share of samples with no answer. A threshold rule takes the statistic, threshold and direction with the highest accuracy on the training problems and applies them to the held-out problem. This test has its own 86 rows: the small-track rows of the 15 October release with complete generations (Qwen3.5-4B 12, Skywork-OR1-Math-7B 12, DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B 10; 24 of 34 robust) and 52 rows of the filtered subset, one per problem (11 robust).

\textbf{Length control.} A logistic regression on four counts of the problem text (log characters, words, digits and \texttt{\$} signs) uses no model at all.

Under split A, 23 of 34 and 32 of 43 test rows have their problem in training. The lookup's gain is the label agreement of Section 1 turned into accuracy. Our own predictors gain at most two rows from split A, so the strict split does not explain their null results. On the small track alone the agreement is 11 of 11 (null 4.7), counting only problems with two or more labelled rows.

\subsection{Problem length and the auxiliary set}

The organisers' example solution, \emph{uncertainty profiling}, generates one greedy response, computes 14 token-level uncertainty features, and fits a random forest whose output is thresholded; its documentation reports balanced accuracy 0.626 on the augmented set. We reran its own selection script (five grouped folds with its seed, its 48-model search, its threshold rule) with the four length counts in place of its features: 0.695, and log characters alone 0.703 (median over the 48 models 0.655; a mean predictor 0.508). Fold assignment depends on row order, so the folds match the organisers' if their feature table kept the dataset's order.

The cause is in the data. All 82 ``change of setting'' rows of the augmented set are non-robust, and their problems are short (median 63 characters, against 120 for robust rows). Log length has AUC 0.74 on the 141 rows, 0.35 on the other 59, and 0.47 and 0.44 on the 34 and 43 competition rows, where the length control scores exactly the majority (Table~\ref{tab:split}). Table~\ref{tab:sources} sets the two label sources side by side.

Table~\ref{tab:signals} and Figure~1 give the results. Calibration comes closest: one row above the baseline on 34 rows and one below on 43. A reviewer noted that its overall rate $p_0$ included the row's own label; computed without the row's problem it gets 26 of 34 (balanced 0.629) and 30 of 43, still below its criterion's 28. On 34 rows the trace, hidden-activation and prompt-capability predictors win no row the baseline loses, so no resample shows a gain; on 43 rows their upper bounds are 2, 0 and 0 rows. Calibration and the prompt-sensitivity and combined variants leave room for a gain of 4 or 5 rows on at least one version, so a small real gain is not excluded. Ranked by score, no signal's held-out AUC interval lies above 0.5; calibration's is 0.67 {[}0.45, 0.95{]} (Appendix B.2). The length control's balanced accuracy on 43 rows (0.577) exceeds every model signal there (best 0.544).

\textbf{Generation stability} (12 rows, exploratory): the largest Spearman correlation with \texttt{max\_drop} is 0.24 (p = 0.46), and the best of the 13 statistics, chosen after the fact, gets 9 of 12 against 8 of 12. In 10 of the 12 rows no original sample finished within 8,192 tokens, so this is a weak test.

\textbf{Forced-answer flips} (86 rows). As first reported, the rule got 52 against a pooled majority of 51, the majority label of all 86 rows taken together. A reviewer found that our answer parser counted an empty \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} (43 of 1,040 answers, in 21 rows) as an answer. Treating those as missing, the rule gets 56, which meets the criterion as written. The gain does not survive a fair baseline: within each label source the rule loses to that source's majority (17 against 24 on the competition rows, 39 against 41 on the filtered subset), and a per-model majority gets 61 of 86. The pooled majority is weak because the two sources have opposite majority labels; the rule mostly learns which source a row came from.

\section{What the results do and do not show}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\tightlist
\item
  \textbf{Nearly every labelled row starts from a solved problem.} In the augmented set and its filtered subset, 138 of 141 and 52 of 54 rows have accuracy 1.0 on the unperturbed problem, and 74 \% and 79 \% of those are non-robust (Appendix C, item 6). A possible reason for the nulls, which we did not test, is that features of one unperturbed prompt see only the solved side of the accuracy difference that defines the label.
\item
  \textbf{Detecting an edit is not detecting an answer change.} The numeric-edit control moved the trace likelihood more than the harmless rewrites in 32 of 42 traced rows, so the trace features see the edits; the contrast still did not separate the labels.
\item
  \textbf{Answer-level tests are far below the labels' scale.} The labels come from sampling at temperature 1.0 with up to 100,000 tokens. Without forcing, DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B gave no final answer within 4,096 tokens on 36 of 36 samples, and Qwen3.5-4B left 10 of 12 problems unanswered at 8,192. A submission has 3,600 seconds for all models and problems, so it cannot repeat the labels' measurement; forced answers at 2,048 tokens are a cheap proxy that did not transfer between label sources.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{Recommendations.} Hold out whole problems; report balanced accuracy and per-model majorities; check public problems against the training labels; and compare any score on the auxiliary set with problem length and with the competition labels.

\section*{Related work}

Our signals follow work on self-evaluation (Kadavath et al., 2022), semantic entropy (Kuhn, Gal and Farquhar, 2023) and hidden-state probes (Azaria and Mitchell, 2023; Burns et al., 2023); our perturbations follow Shi et al.~(2023) and Mirzadeh et al.~(2024). Written chain-of-thought can misreport what drove an answer (Turpin et al., 2023; Lanham et al., 2023), so our trace signal scores a fixed prefix. Repeated subjects inflate predictions in small samples (Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023; Rosenblatt et al., 2024), as our repeated problems do.

\section*{Limitations}

The conclusions concern these six implementations, four locally run models and about 20 to 22 problems; a gain of one or two rows could not be seen, and calibration's interval leaves up to four rows (12 points) unexcluded. Generation stability (12 rows) and forced-answer flips (86 rows) were scored on their own row sets. Prompt features were computed with 16-bit weights; trace and hidden-activation features used 4-bit weights for the three larger models, and the forced-answer flips used 8-bit weights for Skywork and DeepSeek, so all only approximate the organisers' setup. The Olmo-3-7B-Think flips run was stopped at 24 of 108 prompts, so no Olmo row enters that test. Labels exist only outside the organisers' gap between the classes, which can sharpen the agreement of Section 4.1. No signal was tested on gpt-oss-120b or on the public sample. We ran no ablation that intervenes on the model's internals, so we make no claim for the challenge's white-box award, a prize for submissions that show by such an ablation that an internal signal drives their predictions. We built no labels of our own.

\section{Submission history}

Every upload to the competition, in order. A \emph{configuration} is a model at one reasoning-effort setting. \subsection{Method details}\label{sec:B4}

\textbf{Prompt-only features.} One forward pass of the original prompt and of two surface
perturbations (typos, an irrelevant distractor sentence): prompt NLL and length, the change in NLL
under each perturbation, and the cosine change of hidden states at 50 \% and 75 \% depth. A
logistic probe over these eight features.

\textbf{Ability and difficulty calibration.} A per-model ability term and a per-problem difficulty term
estimated from prompt statistics, with the five feature sets of Section 3 and the set chosen inside the outer folds by inner leave-one-problem-out.

\textbf{Generation stability.} Qwen3.5-4B sampled on the originals and perturbed prompts; agreement of the perturbed answers with the original's most common answer, and the change in mean token log-probability, compared with the labels' \texttt{max\_drop}.

\textbf{Counterfactual trace sensitivity.} A deterministic 256-token reasoning prefix is generated from
the original prompt. It is then teacher-forced after the original prompt and after verified
answer-preserving variants: variable renaming (with the rename applied inside the trace),
equivalent numeric expressions, equation-side swaps and reordering of independent givens. A
condition change that alters the answer (one integer constant raised by one) is the negative
control. Eleven features, fixed before the run: the prefix's mean log-probability under the original prompt, the
per-token log-likelihood change (mean, the rewrite type with the largest change,
variance, concentration in the least likely 20 \% of tokens), hidden-state cosine change at 50 \%
and 75 \% depth (also near renamed entities), the ratio of answer-preserving to answer-changing
sensitivity, and indicators for missing rewrite types and a missing control. Only trace positions are
captured, and activations are released per problem.

\section{Further findings}

\begin{enumerate}
\def\labelenumi{\arabic{enumi}.}
\item
  \textbf{The sample is smaller than it looks.} 34 rows from 20 problems give about 20 independent
  units. The intervals of Section 4.1 are 3 to 14 rows wide, so a real gain of one or two
  rows could not be seen here.
\item
  \textbf{Prompt features did not beat the majority.} Capability features reached 23/34, sensitivity
  24/34 and the combined variant 23/34 against a majority of 24 (Section 4). One possible reason is that
  the label is a difference between two accuracies and a feature of one prompt sees only one side
  of it; we did not test this.
\item
  \textbf{Sensitivity of a fixed trace is not sensitivity of the answer.} The trace features measure a
  likelihood response, not whether the final answer survives the perturbation: teacher-forcing
  one reasoning prefix under a renamed or reordered problem measures surprise at the edit. The numeric-edit control moved the likelihood more in 32 of the 42
  traced rows that have one when the raw changes are compared (33 by the ratio feature, whose denominator carries an added 0.001; 49 traced, 7 without a control; median ratio of
  answer-preserving to answer-changing change 0.62; on the 29 labelled rows with a control, 22 of
  29 by the ratio, median 0.62; \texttt{report/trace\_control.py}), so the features see the edits; the contrast still did not separate the labels.
\item
  \textbf{Local models are a proxy for the official deployments.} Qwen3.5-4B ran in BF16; Olmo-3-7B-Think,
  DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B and Skywork-OR1-Math-7B ran with 4-bit (NF4) weights for the prompt, trace and hidden-activation signals, and Skywork and DeepSeek with 8-bit (FP8) weights for the flips. Neither matches the
  organisers' serving setup, and the 4-bit rows may carry quantisation effects in the trace
  features. We report this as a limitation, not as an explanation of the null result.
\item
  \textbf{Hidden states did not help either.} A kernel ridge regression with a linear kernel (a linear probe) on each model's native hidden
  states (prompt and a 128-token reasoning prefix, pooled at 25, 50 and 75 \% depth, model families kept
  in separate coordinates) reached 22/34 under the same nested problem holdout, two below the
  majority (\texttt{fit\_activation\_probe.py}, \texttt{run\_activation\_probe.py}).
\item
  \textbf{The label is about perturbations of solved problems.} In the organisers' augmented set and its filtered subset,
  problems whose every base sample was correct are still mostly non-robust. The two sets are the
  same model, DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B (the organisers' starter repository, \path{uncertainty_profile/config.py}, maps the id
  \texttt{qwen3-8b:low} to it), and all 52 problems of \texttt{augmented-sample-math-agg-filtered} also appear in
  \texttt{augmented-sample-math-agg} (137 problems), so the two figures are not two models. Rows are per perturbation type, so a problem can appear in several rows: the filtered set has
  54 rows over 52 unique \texttt{problem\_id}, the larger set 141 rows over 137. Rows with \texttt{base\_accuracy}
  = 1.0 (any number of base samples, 1 to 60) are 52 of 54 and 138 of 141, nearly all rows, so the
  non-robust share among them (41 of 52, 79 \%; 102 of 138, 74 \%) matches the unconditional share
  (43 of 54, 80 \%; 105 of 141, 74 \%). Restricted to rows with exactly ten base samples: 34 of 40
  (85 \%) and 63 of 82 (77 \%). A script listed in \texttt{REPRODUCE.md} computes all of these. Answer consistency
  on the original cannot be tested in these sets. The public evaluation sets are near balance
  (always ``robust'' scores 0.48 on the main task, and the small-model set is 7 of 14 robust) while our 34
  labelled rows are 71 \% robust (Section 2), so a majority vote learned on the training rows is
  not a safe default.
\item
  \textbf{Answer-level tests run out of token budget.} We started an answer-flip test (each model
  answers the original, typo and distractor copies, and a renamed copy where the problem has a name). DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B
  produced no final answer within 4,096 tokens on 36 of 36 samples of its training problems. This
  check is not reproducible from the archive: its outputs were not kept. We did not run the
  unforced test further; Appendix C, item 8 forces the final answer instead.
\item

  test with the final answer forced: each unfinished sample is continued from its reasoning with
  \texttt{\textless{}/think\textgreater{}} and \texttt{The\ final\ answer\ is\ \textbackslash{}boxed\{} appended, greedy, 24 tokens. Rows: the
  2026-10-15 \texttt{train-main-v2} small-track labels (Qwen3.5-4B 12, Skywork-OR1-Math-7B 12,
  DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B 10; 34 rows, 24 robust) plus the 52 DeepSeek rows of the organisers'

  2,048 tokens, Skywork and DeepSeek with 8-bit floating-point (FP8) weights. Under the same problem-held-out protocol

\end{enumerate}

\textbf{The organisers' example solution, scored once on our labels.} The uncertainty-profiling example is not
one of the six signals; both v1 hybrids shipped it (Appendix A); the v3 submissions ship no example solution. A script listed in \texttt{REPRODUCE.md} generated
greedily with DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B on the 10 DeepSeek rows of the 2026-10-15 train file (10
problems, 7 robust; 4,096 new tokens; the starter repository's pinned library versions; most layers offloaded to the
CPU on a 12 GB card, which changes speed, and greedy tokens only through floating-point differences)
and applied the example solution's estimator and decision threshold unchanged (the saved score file listed in \texttt{REPRODUCE.md}).
It scores 2/10 against a majority of 7/10: balanced accuracy 0.333, difference \textminus{}5 rows with a 95 \%
whole-problem bootstrap interval of {[}\textminus{}10, 0{]}. It predicts non-robust for 9 of 10 rows; both correct
calls are non-robust rows, and the probability that a robust row scores below a non-robust one is
0.14. Ten rows with three non-robust cannot measure its accuracy. They are consistent with a
threshold set where 74.5 \% of rows are non-robust and applied to labels that are 70 \% robust.

Its README documents a grouped out-of-fold balanced accuracy of 0.6262 and plain accuracy 0.6879
(starter repository, \path{solutions/uncertainty-profiling/README.md}, lines 127-128; DeepSeek only, on the organisers'
augmented set). That is below the 0.70 balanced accuracy our trace and activation criteria required, so it would not
meet a success criterion either. A constant ``non-robust'' scores 105/141 = 0.745 on that set, above the
example solution's 97/141. Our ten rows count against it, and the v2 submissions that use it were not uploaded.

A length baseline (\texttt{report/length\_baseline.py}) tests the 0.626 against problem length. On the
same 141 rows (137 problems), five-fold problem-grouped folds, seeds 0 to 19, a standardized
logistic regression with balanced class weights on four features of the problem text (log
character count, word count, digit count, \texttt{\$} count) reaches balanced accuracy 0.644 (0.592 to
0.677) and accuracy 0.664; log character count alone reaches 0.698 (accuracy 0.702). A
label-shuffle null (200 shuffles that permute labels across problems, rows of one problem kept
together, one seed each) has median 0.498, 95th percentile 0.592 and maximum 0.647. Under these folds the comparison is indicative; Section 4.2 repeats it with the example solution's own selection script, where length reaches 0.695 and 0.703.

Transfer to the organisers' public sample (\texttt{report/sample\_transfer.py}): labelling the 558-row sample by the outline in the challenge's proposal paper (linked from the competition page), which the organisers say differs from the official rule
(largest \texttt{absolute\_accuracy\_decay} per model and problem, robust at most 0.10, non-robust from 0.25), gives 69 labelled
(model, problem) pairs of 80 over 10 problems, 38 robust. Five of the 10 problems are also in \texttt{train-main-v2}, and only
\texttt{gpt-oss-120b:low} and \texttt{qwen3-8b:low} (taken to be DeepSeek-R1-0528-Qwen3-8B) match a training configuration; the per-configuration prior
predicts robust for both, so it scores 8 of 15 labelled pairs, the same as the pooled majority. Our six signals need model features
that were never computed for these problems (a GPU run), so none was tested there.
